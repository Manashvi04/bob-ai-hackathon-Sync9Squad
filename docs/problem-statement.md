# Problem Statement

## Background

Global ports are critical hubs in international supply chains, handling large volumes of vessels and containers every day. Port operators must coordinate limited resources such as berths, cranes, yard space, and operational shifts while vessel arrival schedules continuously change.

When several vessels arrive within the same time window or port resources become heavily utilised, congestion can quickly develop. Delays at one berth can create cascading effects across crane allocation, container handling, yard capacity, vessel queues, and subsequent port operations.

The 2021 Los Angeles/Long Beach port backlog demonstrated the scale of this problem, with 100+ ships waiting offshore for weeks and an estimated impact of more than $10 billion on global supply chains.

## The Problem

Port operators often allocate berths, cranes, and yard space across hundreds of vessels manually using spreadsheets and operational experience.

Congestion hotspots are frequently identified reactively — only after vessels have already started queueing or port resources are approaching their limits.

This creates several operational challenges:

- Multiple vessels competing for limited berth capacity
- Inefficient crane allocation
- Increasing vessel waiting and turnaround time
- Yard areas approaching critical utilisation
- Resource conflicts caused by changing vessel schedules
- Alternate routing or rescheduling decisions being made too late
- Difficulty creating an efficient short-term operations plan when conditions change

Port operators therefore need a system that can identify congestion risks in advance and convert those predictions into practical operational recommendations.

## Who is Affected

The primary users affected by this problem are port operations teams responsible for coordinating vessel and terminal activities, particularly:

- Port operations managers
- Berth planners
- Crane and terminal planners
- Yard supervisors
- Shift supervisors
- Vessel operations teams
- Shipping lines and logistics coordinators

For shift supervisors in particular, the challenge is not simply knowing that the port is busy. They need to understand which vessels or resources are creating the bottleneck, what is likely to happen during the next operating window, and what action should be taken.

## Why It Matters

Port congestion has effects far beyond the port itself.

When vessels wait offshore or remain at berths longer than expected, the consequences can include:

- Increased vessel waiting time
- Higher fuel and operating costs
- Reduced berth and crane utilisation efficiency
- Container handling delays
- Yard congestion
- Delayed cargo delivery
- Disruption to downstream trucking, warehousing, and logistics operations
- Cascading delays across global supply chains

The challenge statement highlights the 2021 Los Angeles/Long Beach backlog, where more than 100 vessels waited offshore for weeks and global supply-chain costs were estimated at over $10 billion.

Even smaller congestion events can become expensive because a delay involving one vessel or berth can affect several later operations.

Predicting these bottlenecks before they occur gives port operators more time to adjust vessel schedules, redistribute resources, and consider alternate routing strategies.

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short

Port planning is still heavily dependent on spreadsheets, static schedules, manual coordination, and the experience of port operators.

These approaches can work under normal operating conditions but become difficult when vessel schedules and resource availability change rapidly.

Traditional monitoring dashboards also tend to focus on the current state of the port. They may show berth occupancy, vessel queues, or yard utilisation, but identifying an existing bottleneck is different from predicting one early enough to prevent it.

A useful solution therefore needs to go beyond monitoring.

It should combine vessel schedules and port-capacity information to:

1. Predict upcoming congestion hotspots
2. Identify the resources contributing to the risk
3. Recommend better berth and crane assignments
4. Suggest alternate routing or rescheduling strategies
5. Generate a practical 72-hour operations plan for shift supervisors

This enables port operations to move from **reactive congestion management to proactive operational planning**.
